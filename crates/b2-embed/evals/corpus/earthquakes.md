Stress builds for decades along a locked fault until the rock suddenly slips,
releasing the stored strain as seismic waves that shake the ground for miles.
Magnitude scales run logarithmically, so each whole step is roughly thirty
times the energy of the last. Aftershocks trail the main rupture for days as
the crust settles into its new position.

## Faults and the cycle

A fault is a fracture where two blocks of crust move past each other, and the
direction of that motion sorts them into families. Where the crust is pulling
apart one block drops relative to the other; where it is being squeezed one
block rides up over its neighbor; where two blocks grind sideways the motion is
horizontal and the classic long, straight surface trace appears across the
landscape. The great subduction boundaries, where an oceanic plate dives
beneath a continental one, are the compressional case at continental scale, and
they produce the largest events on record.

Most of a fault's life is spent stuck. Friction across the interface holds it
in place while the plates keep moving at the pace of a growing fingernail, so
elastic strain accumulates in the surrounding rock like tension in a bent stick.
Slip happens when the accumulated stress finally exceeds what friction can
resist, and the rock on either side snaps back toward its unstrained shape. That
rebound is the earthquake, and it is why the shaking radiates from a rupture
surface rather than from a single point.

Not every fault behaves this way. Some segments creep steadily instead of
locking, releasing their budget in a continuous, unfelt slide that gradually
offsets curbs and fence lines. Creeping segments are often the boundaries that
stop a rupture from propagating further, which is one reason the same fault
system can produce a modest event in one decade and a regional catastrophe in
another.

## Waves and what they do

The rupture radiates two kinds of body wave through the interior. The first is
a compression that travels fastest and arrives as a sharp jolt or a bang; the
second shears the rock sideways, travels slower, and arrives with far stronger
sideways motion than the first. Behind both come surface waves, which are
slowest of all, decay least with distance, and often carry the largest motion a
seismogram records — the long rolling that topples tall buildings far from the
source. Which wave does the damage is a property of the building as much as
the wave: radiated energy, ground amplitude, and a structure's own resonance
are three different quantities, and conflating them predicts the wrong ruins.

Ground conditions can matter more than distance. Loose sediment in a river
valley or a filled bay amplifies slow shaking severalfold compared with bedrock
a few kilometers away, so damage maps often trace old geology rather than
proximity to the fault. Saturated sand can lose its strength entirely under
repeated shaking and behave as a liquid, letting buried tanks float upward and
buildings settle and tilt while remaining otherwise intact.

| Magnitude | Energy equivalent | Global frequency | Shaking near the rupture |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.0–3.9 | A quarry blast | ~100,000 a year | Felt indoors as a passing truck |
| 4.0–4.9 | 1 kiloton | ~13,000 a year | Dishes rattle, hanging objects swing |
| 5.0–5.9 | 32 kilotons | ~1,500 a year | Plaster cracks, unbraced chimneys fall |
| 6.0–6.9 | 1 megaton | ~130 a year | Weak masonry fails, slopes give way |
| 7.0–7.9 | 32 megatons | ~15 a year | Heavy structural damage across a wide area |
| 8.0 and up | A gigaton and beyond | About 1 a year | Visible surface rupture, regional destruction |

Two numbers get confused constantly. Magnitude is a single property of the
event itself — how much rock moved, how far, over how large a surface — and an
earthquake has exactly one. Intensity describes the experience at a particular
place and varies enormously across a single event, which is why a distant
observer and someone standing over the rupture will honestly report completely
different things about the same shock.

## Forecasting, and its limits

Nobody can say that a specific fault will break on a specific day, and a
century of trying has produced no reliable short-term precursor. Radon in well
water, animal behavior, and small earthquake swarms all look suggestive after
the fact and fail as predictors before it — a swarm gets renamed a foreshock
sequence only in hindsight, once a larger rupture has followed it — because the
same signals appear far more often with nothing following.

What does work is the long view. Trenching across a fault exposes layers offset
by past ruptures, and dating them establishes how often that segment has broken
over thousands of years. Combining that record with the measured rate of strain
accumulation gives a probability over decades — the basis for building codes,
insurance pricing, and where a hospital may be sited. It is genuinely useful and
it is not a forecast of tomorrow.

## Early warning

Detection can nonetheless outrun the damaging waves, because electronics travel
at light speed and rock does not. A dense sensor network recognizes the fast,
weak compression arriving at stations nearest the source, estimates the size and
location within a second or two, and pushes an alert outward that overtakes the
slower shear and surface waves still on their way. The lead time is small and
strictly geometric: nothing at all directly above the rupture, a few seconds at
thirty kilometers, tens of seconds at a hundred.

That is enough to matter. Trains brake, elevators stop and open at the nearest
floor, surgeons lift their hands, valves close on gas mains, and a phone on a
nightstand a hundred kilometers away buzzes long enough before the roll arrives
for someone to get under a table. Most deployments tune their thresholds
toward false alarms rather than misses — a per-system choice that gets
revisited, not a universal rule — on the reasoning that an unnecessary alert
costs a few seconds and a missed one costs considerably more.
