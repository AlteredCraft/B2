## Tire pressure

Road tires run higher pressure than the rim's printed max suggests in your
head — 80 to 90 psi doesn't ride "hard," it just transmits less rolling
resistance because the tire itself flexes less, not because it's a harsher
ride at speed. Wider tires flip that intuition: a wide tire run soft often
rolls faster than a narrow one run hard, because the lower pressure lets
the tire deform around road texture instead of bouncing over it, and the
energy that bounce wastes costs more than the marginal drag of the wider
contact patch. The practical range worth knowing: a heavier rider on a
narrower tire pushes toward the top of the printed range, a lighter rider
on a wider tire toward the bottom, checked with a real gauge since a
thumb-squeeze can't tell 70 psi from 90.

## Knots worth knowing cold

Six knots cover almost everything a small-boat sailor needs, and the
difference between knowing them and half-remembering them under a slapping
sail is the difference between a boat that holds and one that doesn't. The
bowline ties a fixed loop that won't slip under load and unties easily even
after being loaded hard — tie it wrong-handed once under pressure and
you'll never trust it again, so it's worth drilling one-handed and behind
your back until the mnemonic is muscle memory rather than words. A cleat
hitch takes a line under load without ever fully locking it: a figure-eight
wrap, then a final hitch with a twist that traps itself against the last
wrap, releasable in a hurry even under full tension — the mistake beginners
make is adding a second locking hitch, which turns an instantly-releasable
knot into one that has to be worked loose by hand while the boat drags on
it. A round turn and two half hitches secures a line to a post or ring
under a load that varies in direction, the round turn taking the initial
shock before the half hitches lock it off.

Standing rigging is the wire that holds the mast up and, aside from
periodic tension checks, isn't meant to move; running rigging is everything
that does move — halyards raising sails, sheets trimming them, and the
various control lines that shape a sail's curve once it's up. Halyard
tension shapes draft position more than most new sailors expect: a loose
halyard lets the deepest part of the sail's curve drift aft, which is slow
and hard to control upwind, while cranking it tight enough pulls that draft
forward again — visible as horizontal wrinkles radiating from the leading
edge when it's overdone. Sheet tension is the one every new sailor
over-trims: a sail sheeted in too hard stalls like a wing at too steep an
angle, and the fix is almost always to ease until a slight luff appears at
the front edge, then trim back just past the point it stops luffing. A boat
that feels sluggish and won't point upwind is more often over-trimmed than
under-powered — the instinct to pull everything tighter when a boat feels
slow is usually exactly backwards.
