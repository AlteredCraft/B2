A squeaking drivetrain is a dry one: degrease the chain, re-lube it, and wipe
the excess so grit doesn't stick. Sloppy or skipping gear changes usually trace
to cable tension — a quarter turn of the barrel adjuster at the derailleur
trues up the indexing. Keep tires at the pressure printed on the sidewall and
replace brake pads before the wear groove disappears.

## The drivetrain

Lubrication is a smaller job than most people make it and a more frequent one.
A drop on each roller, a slow backpedal to distribute it, and a firm wipe with a
rag is the whole procedure; the film that matters lives inside the rollers and
pins, and everything left on the outside is simply an adhesive for road grit.
Wet lubricants survive rain and attract dirt, dry ones stay clean and wash off
in the first downpour, and choosing between them is a question about local
weather rather than about quality.

Shifting problems fall into two groups that feel similar and are fixed
differently. Hesitation that is equally bad across every gear is a cable
problem: housing that has compressed, a cable that has stretched, or friction
from contamination inside the run. Trouble that appears only at one end of the
cassette is a limit-screw or hanger problem, and the hanger is the usual
culprit — it is deliberately the softest part of the frame so that it bends and
sacrifices itself instead of the derailleur in a fall.

Elongation is the measurement that decides when the chain gets replaced, and it
is not really stretch. The rollers and pins wear, each link picks up a fraction
of a millimeter of slop, and the accumulated pitch grows until the chain no
longer sits correctly in the teeth it was meshing with. A gauge dropped into
the links reads the figure directly. Replace at 0.75 percent on a modern narrow
chain and the cassette survives to see several chains; let it run past 1
percent and the worn chain reshapes every sprocket it touches, turning a cheap
consumable into an expensive one. A new chain that skips under load on an old
cassette is the diagnosis arriving too late.

## Brakes

Rim pads glaze over with a hard, shiny layer that squeals and grips poorly, and
a few passes with sandpaper restores them faster than replacement. Check them
for embedded metal slivers at the same time, since a single fragment will score
a rim through in a season. Toe-in — setting the leading edge to contact
fractionally first — is the traditional cure for squeal and still works.

Disc systems fail differently. Contamination is the dominant problem, and even
a fingerprint of chain lubricant on a rotor produces a howl and a dramatic loss
of power that no amount of adjustment recovers; the pads have to be replaced and
the rotor cleaned with alcohol. A lever that feels spongy through its whole
travel has air in a hydraulic line and needs bleeding, while a lever that pulls
progressively closer to the bar over weeks is simply reporting pad wear.

## Wheels and tires

A wheel is a pre-tensioned structure, not a rigid one, and it holds its shape
because every spoke is pulling inward against a rim that resists compression.
Riding a wheel with two or three loose spokes cycles the remainder through a
much larger stress range and fatigues them at the elbow, which is why one
broken spoke is so often followed by another within a month.

Truing is the art of correcting a wobble by adjusting tension rather than by
forcing the rim over. Turn the nipple at the high spot to pull the rim toward
the opposite side, work in quarter turns, and check dish and roundness as well
as side-to-side, because chasing one of the three while ignoring the others
produces a wheel that looks straight and is under wildly uneven tension.

Tire pressure is the single adjustment with the largest effect on how a bike
rides, and the sidewall number is a maximum rather than a recommendation.
Heavier riders need more, wider tires need dramatically less, and rough surfaces
reward running lower than feels intuitive, because a tire that deforms around a
bump loses less speed than a rigid one that lifts the whole bicycle over it.

| Fastener or setting | Figure | Consequence of getting it wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Left crank-arm pinch bolts, Shimano Hollowtech II, tightened alternately (other crank systems: use the maker's figure) | 12–14 N·m | Loose arms round out the spindle interface |
| Cassette lockring | 40 N·m | Under-tight rings unwind and destroy the freehub |
| Stem faceplate bolts | 5 N·m | Over-torque crushes carbon bars, under-torque slips |
| Disc rotor bolts | 6 N·m | Loose rotors wobble, warp, and drag |
| Spoke tension, rear drive side | 100–120 kgf | Low tension fatigues elbows and breaks spokes |
| Road tire, 25 mm, 75 kg rider | 80 psi | Too high skitters over rough ground |
| Gravel tire, 40 mm, 75 kg rider | 35 psi | Too low invites pinch flats and burping |

The pattern in the right-hand column is worth more than the numbers themselves:
almost every entry fails badly in both directions, which is why a torque wrench
earns its place on a bench far more than any other single tool. Aluminum
threads and carbon clamping surfaces have no forgiveness in either direction,
and the traditional practice of pulling everything up hard came from steel
frames and steel fasteners that did.

## A rhythm rather than a project

Maintenance that happens on a schedule stays small. A wipe and a lube of the
chain every few rides, a squeeze of the tires before each one, and a minute
spent lifting each wheel and spinning it to listen for rub catches most
developing faults while they are still adjustments. Every few months, pull the
seatpost, check the headset for play by holding the front brake and rocking the
bike, and look at the pads and the chain gauge together.

The alternative is the annual overhaul, which reliably discovers that three
consumables were run past their limits and took a more expensive part with them
each time. Bicycles are unusually honest machines: nearly everything that will
eventually strand a rider announces itself first as a small noise or a slight
change in feel, and the entire discipline is a habit of noticing.
