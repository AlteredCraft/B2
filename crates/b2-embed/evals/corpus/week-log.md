## Hive check

The bees drew out most of the new frames in the second box, which answers the
question of whether the colony was ready for the extra space — they were, and
the outer combs are already half filled with capped stores. Brood of every age
across the middle five frames: eggs standing upright in polished
cells, larvae glistening, and a laying arrangement so even it looks stamped,
so the queen is doing her job without needing to be spotted. Smoker technique
is finally clicking — two puffs at the entrance, a full minute's wait before
lifting the cover, and the whole inspection stays calm enough to work without
gloves. Propolis everywhere this time of year; the hive tool wants scraping
after every other frame or it glues itself to your hand. Drone comb along the
bottom bars again — cut a patch out for the varroa check and the count was
reassuringly low, single digits on the tray after three days. Made a note to
reverse the boxes in spring rather than now: the winter ball of bees moves
upward through the stores, and reversing too early splits the brood nest in
two just when they can least afford it. Total time in the hive, eleven
minutes. The old advice holds that every minute past ten costs the colony a
day of composure, which is probably folklore, but it keeps an inspection
disciplined, and a disciplined inspection is one you actually do every week
instead of putting off.

## The knife drawer

An afternoon with the whetstones, overdue by months. The 1000-grit does the
real work: raise a burr along the full length of the edge, feel for it with a
thumb dragged sideways off the spine side, then flip the blade and match it
from the other face. The burr is the whole job — no burr means the two
bevels have not met yet, and polishing an edge that never met is how you
spend an hour making a dull knife shiny. Fifteen degrees per side for the
thin Japanese blade, closer to twenty for the beat-up German one that gets
used on squash and frozen things it was never meant for. The 6000-grit stone
afterwards with gentle alternating passes, then a strop on the leather paddle
to chase off the last of the wire edge. The marker stripe kept the angle
honest: colour the bevel, take two passes, and wherever the stripe survives
the stone never touched — the feedback is instant and it does not flatter.
Tomato test at the end: the skin should part under the blade's own weight
with the barest slicing motion, no pressure. The 400-grit stone only comes
out for chips, since it removes metal fast enough to eat a blade away
over the years if it becomes a habit. The soaking stones want their ten minutes in the tub
before use; the hard resin one is splash-and-go. Note for next time: the
paring knife's tip has a rolled section right at the point that the flat
stones never touch — it needs a slip stone or a different fixture entirely.

## Warblers at the reservoir

Up before daybreak for the loop around the reservoir, and the effort paid:
eleven warbler species by nine o'clock, four of them new for the year.
Yellow-rumped everywhere, as expected — though the field mark you actually
use is the sharp chip note and the habit of sallying out after insects like a
flycatcher, not the rump patch the guides advertise. One black-throated blue
working the low shrubs gave the kind of view binoculars were invented for:
male, unmistakable, the small white wing square showing even in deep shade.
Relearned that the ear names the bird long before the eye gets involved —
the prairie warbler had announced itself from the willows by the inlet a
full minute before it showed, and the redstart did the same from the
tangle by the outflow. The 8x42s remain the right
compromise: a field of view wide enough to find a moving bird in the canopy,
bright enough in the understory, not so much magnification that every
heartbeat becomes a wobble. Kept the tally in the notebook's back pages with
time and weather, because the lists only become interesting years later and
only if they were honest when written. Missed the waterthrush two other
birders reported from earlier — heard something promising near the inlet but
never pinned it down, and counting a maybe is how the whole list goes quietly
soft. Next visit: the far shore an hour earlier, before the joggers push
everything up into the treetops.

## Sketching practice

Third week of the daily ten-minute sketch, and proportion is finally coming
under control. The trick that unlocked it: measure everything against the
first shape put down on the page — hold the pencil out at full stretch, lock
the elbow, and read widths and heights off the thumb like a ruler. The bandstand
across the square took four attempts before the awning stopped floating free
of its posts. Perspective errors announce themselves loudest in windows: let
one vanishing point slide and every opening on the facade leans in sympathy,
so the ruler-check is worth the interruption. Gesture first, structure
second, detail last — the drawings that fail are the ones that started with a
doorknob. 2B for the block-in, 4B for the deepest tones, and the eraser used
as a drawing tool, pulling pale shapes back out of a toned patch rather than
apologising for mistakes. Values read better squinted: half-close the eyes
and the scene collapses into four tones, which is all a small sketch can hold
anyway. People remain the weak point — any sitter who holds still gets rendered
stiff as furniture, so the new rule is thirty-second figures only, no faces,
until the stiffness leaves. The sketchbook's first pages already look like
someone else's work, which is the entire argument for dating every page and
never tearing one out, however deserving.

## A day on the lava field

Drove out to the national monument and spent the whole day on the lava
field, and the scale of it rearranges you. The plain is built from repeated
flows: molten rock reaches the surface through a rupture in the crust, loses
its gases, and hardens, and each episode lays another sheet down on the last
— the cone at the field's centre is nothing but those layers stacked,
outburst after outburst, until the pile stands above everything around it.
You can read the sequence in the crater wall like tree rings: bands of rough
cindery rubble from the violent episodes, smooth ropy sheets from the quiet
ones, thin beds of ash pressed between them. The ranger at the fissure line
explained that the vents along it fed the youngest flows — the ropy surface
underfoot, barely weathered — while the jagged clinker fields to the east
came from older activity, already crumbling into soil at their margins. The
gases matter more than I had realised: it is dissolved gas coming out of
solution that decides whether molten rock arrives as a quiet flow that
spreads and hardens in sheets, or goes up as a column of ash that falls back
to build the cone's abrupt flanks. Standing on the crater floor where the rock
once welled up glowing, with the cooled sheets of it spreading to the horizon,
the eye keeps trying to file the landscape as scenery and failing — it is a
process caught mid-stride, paused rather than finished, and the notice by
the gate saying the last activity was within living memory does concentrate
the attention.

## Candle batch

Second batch of soy candles, better than the first in every measurable way.
The lesson from round one was that the wick does most of the deciding: the
same wax in the same jar with one size larger wick moved from tunnelling — a
melt pool that never reaches the glass, leaving a chimney of unburned wax —
to a full pool in forty minutes. Poured at sixty degrees this time instead
of straight off the double boiler, and the tops set flat instead of sinking
around the wick as the first batch did. Fragrance oil at eight percent,
stirred in for a slow two minutes so it binds with the wax before the
temperature drops — the step batch one skipped, and likely why its throw
stayed faint. Wicked the jars with a dab from the glue gun and centred each wick
with two clothespins laid across the rim, which is inelegant and works
perfectly. The burn test has a protocol now: four hours minimum, melt pool
diameter noted each hour, and an eye kept on mushrooming at the wick tip — a
trim to a quarter inch fixes it, and an untrimmed mushroom is where the soot
comes from. The curing question stays open: the forums swear two weeks in a
closed cupboard deepens the scent throw, and batch one out of the cupboard
does smell fuller than it did fresh, but nobody seems to have run the
comparison with the same nose on the same day. Batch three will hold half
the jars back and check.

## Dovetails for the cedar box

Hand-cut dovetails in cedar, and the first joint of mine that closes without
mallet-shaped persuasion. Tails first, gang-cut with both boards in the
vise, which guarantees the two sides agree with each other even where they
disagree with the plan. The revelation was the marking gauge: score the
baseline deep enough to register a chisel tip in, and the shoulder stops
wandering because the tool has a physical wall to drop into rather than a
pencil stroke to interpret. Sawing to the line means leaving the line —
split the stroke's thickness and the joint gets snugger by exactly that
much. The waste came out with shallow chisel taps from each face, meeting in
the middle so neither face blows out, and the final paring cut taken
standing directly over the work, where vertical reads honestly. Cedar is
soft enough to crush if the chisel arrives with ambition, so the pin cheeks
got pared in thin curls with hand pressure alone, always with the grain
running away from the baseline. Transfer decides everything: clamp the tail
board over the pin board's end grain and mark with the knife, unhurried,
because the pins can only ever be as true as those knifed marks. Dry fit,
look for daylight in the joint, and pare only the pins — the tails are the
reference and stay untouched once cut. The box this is all for gets glued up
at the weekend; the offcut with the test joint in it is going on the shelf
above the bench, the way you keep a first pancake.
