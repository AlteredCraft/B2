The habits that set up a good night: going to bed and getting up at the same
hour every day, keeping the bedroom dark, quiet, and slightly cool, and winding
down with a screen-free routine — reading, stretching, dim light — for the last
half hour. The bed is reserved for sleeping, so the mind learns that lying down
means switching off rather than scrolling or working.

## Why regularity does the heavy lifting

Two separate systems decide when a person feels sleepy, and habits work by
keeping them pointed the same direction. The first is a pressure that builds
steadily across every waking hour and discharges only during rest, which is why
a long day makes the first part of the night deep and unbroken. The second is
an internal clock running on its own near-daily cycle, which decides *when* the
pressure is allowed to feel overwhelming — and it is the clock, not the
pressure, that habits actually train.

A fixed wake time is the stronger of the two anchors, and it is the one people
neglect. Bedtime is largely an outcome: rise at the same hour for a week and the
evening takes care of itself, because pressure accumulates on schedule. Fixing
bedtime while letting the morning drift does the reverse, producing an hour of
staring at the ceiling and the mistaken conclusion that the capacity for rest
has broken down, when the real problem is that too few waking hours have
accumulated yet.

Sleeping in on a rest day undoes more than it repays. Shifting the wake time by
two or three hours moves the internal clock westward as surely as a flight
would, and the following night arrives before the body believes it should — the
Sunday-evening version of jet lag, produced without leaving the house. The
repair for a short week is a modest, early afternoon nap rather than a long
morning, since a nap discharges some pressure without touching the clock at all.

## The bedroom as equipment

Treating the room as a piece of equipment rather than as decoration is a useful
reframing. Every input that reaches a sleeping person — light through a gap in
the curtains, a partner's alarm, a heating system cycling on at four — has an
effect whether or not it produces a remembered awakening, and brief arousals
that never reach consciousness still fragment the night and show up as daytime
fog with no obvious cause.

Sound is best handled by masking rather than by elimination, because the waking
trigger is contrast rather than volume: a steady fan defeats a slamming door far
better than silence does. Light is the opposite and wants genuine removal, since
even modest illumination reaching closed eyelids suppresses the evening hormone
that signals nightfall. Temperature is the input people most often get wrong,
and it is the one with the clearest number attached.

| Hours before bed | What to do | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | Finish hard exercise | Core temperature needs hours to fall again |
| 3 | Finish the last large meal | Digestion and reflux fragment the first cycle |
| 2 | Set the thermostat to 16 to 19 °C | The body must shed heat to initiate sleep |
| 2 | Drop the household lighting to lamps | Overhead light delays the evening hormone |
| 1 | Screens away, dim reading only | Removes both the light and the alerting content |
| 0 | Bed, at the same hour, in a dark room | The anchor everything else is arranged around |

Falling asleep depends on the body dumping about a degree of core temperature,
which it does by pushing blood to the hands and feet. A cool room accelerates
that transfer and a warm one blocks it — which explains the otherwise
contradictory advice that a hot bath ninety minutes before bed helps, since the
rebound afterward drops core temperature faster than doing nothing at all.

## Light is the main lever

The clock is set almost entirely by light hitting the eye, and it responds to
quantity in a way indoor life systematically underdelivers. A well-lit office
supplies a few hundred lux; a completely overcast morning outdoors supplies
around 10,000 lux, and direct sun many times more. Fifteen or twenty minutes
outside shortly after rising is therefore a far stronger signal than an entire
day at a desk beside a window, and it is the single most effective thing
available to someone whose schedule keeps drifting later.

The same sensitivity runs in reverse at night. Evening light delays the clock,
which is the mechanism behind the standard advice about screens — though the
timing and the brightness matter considerably more than the color temperature
does, and an hour of any bright light before bed outweighs the effect a blue
filter is meant to correct.

## When the habits are not the problem

Hygiene is genuinely preventive and it is a weak treatment. Someone who has
already spent months lying awake has usually formed an association between the
bed and frustrated wakefulness, and no amount of thermostat adjustment breaks a
learned association. The evidence-backed response is behavioral rather than
environmental: get out of bed when sleep does not come, use it only when
genuinely sleepy, and compress time in bed until it is nearly all sleeping,
then expand it again.

Snoring with witnessed pauses, an irresistible urge to move the legs in the
evening, or persistent sleepiness despite adequate opportunity are all signals
that point away from habits entirely and toward something a clinician should
look at. The distinction matters, because months of diligent routine-fixing is a
poor substitute for diagnosing a disorder that routine cannot reach.
