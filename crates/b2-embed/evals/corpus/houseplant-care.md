Most indoor plants die of kindness: overwatering drowns the pot, so let the top
of the soil dry before watering again and always use a container that drains.
Place ferns and other tropicals away from radiators and direct sun — bright,
indirect light and a bit of humidity keep fronds from crisping and browning at
the tips. Yellowing lower leaves usually mean soggy soil, not thirst.

## Watering by weight, not by calendar

A schedule is the wrong instrument because the same pot needs water at wildly
different intervals in July and January. Weight is the right one: lift the pot
after a thorough soak and again a week later, and the difference is
unmistakable once felt a few times. A finger pushed a couple of centimetres
into the soil answers the same question more crudely, and a cheap moisture
probe answers it worse than either — the inexpensive ones drift with dissolved
salts, soil composition, and calibration, so the needle is only loosely about
water.

Watering thoroughly and infrequently beats little-and-often. A full soak until
liquid runs from the drainage holes wets the entire root ball and flushes
accumulated mineral salts out with it; a daily splash wets only the top
centimetre, so the roots stay shallow and the salts concentrate steadily. Empty
the saucer twenty minutes afterward, because the drowning that kills plants is
almost always a pot standing in its own runoff rather than the watering itself.

Tap water is fine for the overwhelming majority of species, its chlorine
harmless at municipal concentrations. The exceptions are worth knowing —
carnivorous plants, and a handful of prayer plants and their relatives, are
genuinely sensitive to fluoride and dissolved minerals, and they respond to
rainwater, distilled, or reverse-osmosis water with a visible reduction in
brown tipping — an ordinary charcoal jug filter does not take fluoride out.

## Light, honestly measured

Human vision adapts so aggressively that rooms people describe as bright are
routinely an order of magnitude too dim for growth. The reliable test costs
nothing: hold a hand a foot above the leaves at midday and look at the shadow.
A crisp shadow with sharp edges is direct sun, a soft shadow with recognizable
outline is bright indirect, and a shadow that is barely discernible is low
light, whatever the room feels like.

Distance from the glass matters more than compass direction, because
illuminance falls off sharply as you step back. A plant a metre inside a bright
window is receiving a small fraction of what one on the sill receives, and the
difference between the sill and the far wall is larger than the difference
between any two windows in the house.

The figures below describe one temperate window with no supplemental lighting —
species, season, glazing, and the room itself all move them, so treat the rows
as starting points rather than instructions:

| Position | Character of the light | Summer watering | Suitable plants |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unobstructed south sill | Direct sun for most of the day | Every 3–5 days | Succulents, cacti, citrus |
| A metre back from that window | Bright and indirect | Weekly | Monstera, rubber plant, fig |
| East-facing sill | Gentle direct sun each morning | Weekly | Ferns, calathea, peace lily |
| Interior of a north-facing room | Low, never direct | Every 10–14 days | Snake plant, ZZ, pothos |
| Over three metres from any glass | Below the threshold for growth, absent a grow lamp | Not applicable | Nothing survives long term unaided |

The bottom row is the one people argue with, and it is the one that is hardest
to escape. A plant in genuine darkness is not growing slowly; it is spending
stored reserves and shrinking, and it will look acceptable for months while
doing so. The workable answer for a dark corner is a rotation — two identical
plants swapping places every few weeks — or a modest grow light, which has
become cheap enough that it is no longer an enthusiast's solution.

## Reading the leaves

Symptoms are ambiguous in a specific and learnable way. Yellowing that starts
with the lowest leaves and spreads upward, on soil that stays damp, is
overwatering. Yellowing that begins at the outer edges on soil pulling away
from the pot is the opposite. Crisp brown edges with a thin yellow halo point
to dry air or accumulated salts, while soft brown patches in the middle of a
leaf point to a fungal problem and too little airflow.

Pale, stretched growth with long gaps between leaves is a plant reaching for a
window, and it is the only symptom in this list that no watering change will
fix. Sudden loss of several leaves at once after a move usually means nothing
worse than acclimation, since many species shed what they cannot support at
their new light level and then stabilize.

Pests announce themselves late, so the habit worth building is turning a few
leaves over whenever you water. Spider mites are the ones that do real damage
before being noticed — almost too small to resolve, they leave a faint stippling
across the upper surface and fine strands where the stems meet. They thrive
precisely in the warm, dry air of a heated winter room, which is why an
infestation so often follows the radiators coming on. Sticky residue on a leaf
or the surface below it means scale or aphids above it, and white cottony tufts
in the leaf joints are mealybugs.

## Repotting and dormancy

Roots circling the inside of the pot, water running straight through, or a
plant drying out within a day or two mean it has outgrown its container. Move
up one size only — a plant swamped in a much larger pot sits in soil that stays
wet far longer than its roots can use, which is the overwatering failure
arriving by a different route.

Winter changes the rules for anything resting in natural light. Growth slows
or stops for most plants, water use falls sharply, and feeding should stop for
a plant that has gone quiet — though one still growing under a lamp keeps its
summer appetite, and the rules above keep applying to it. The most common
January mistake is continuing a summer routine into a season the plant cannot
use it in. Cutting watering back as the pot stays wet longer, and moving pots
nearer the glass, compensates for the lower light — it does nothing for dry
heated air, and glass on a freezing night radiates a chill of its own, so a
sensitive plant wants close to the light without touching the pane.
