## Marathon training

The long run is the session everything else in the week is arranged around,
not because it burns the most calories but because it's the only workout
that teaches the body to spare glycogen and lean on fat for fuel once the
easy miles run out — a skill that only shows up past roughly ninety
minutes, which is why a training plan cares about the long run's duration
more than its distance. Tapering feels wrong while you're doing it: cutting
volume by half two weeks out and further the final week, when the fitness
already banked doesn't evaporate that fast but the accumulated fatigue
does, and runners who skip the taper because they feel undertrained
routinely race worse than ones who trusted it. Pace discipline in the early
miles matters more than anywhere else in the race — starting even ten
seconds per mile too fast feels effortless at mile three and costs two or
three minutes at mile twenty, when the deficit compounds instead of
averaging out. Easy days are supposed to feel almost too slow; the runners
who turn every run into a moderate effort are the ones who plateau, because
the adaptation from a hard session only sticks if the days around it are
genuinely easy enough to absorb it.

## An aquarium log

A tank that looks spotless the day it's filled is the most dangerous state
it will ever be in, because the bacteria colony that actually makes water
safe hasn't grown yet — cycling a filter takes four to six weeks of dosing
ammonia and testing daily while nothing living is in the tank, and skipping
that wait is the single most common way a new hobbyist kills an entire
first stock of fish within days. Once the colony is established, ammonia
and nitrite should both read zero and the only number that climbs between
water changes is nitrate, which is the whole point: a twenty to thirty
percent weekly water change is really a nitrate dilution schedule, not a
cleaning ritual, and a tank that's never had one accumulates nitrate until
even hardy fish stop thriving for reasons that don't show up as an obvious
illness. Stocking too fast is the second-most common mistake, since the
bacteria colony sizes itself to the current waste load and a sudden jump in
fish count outpaces it long before the tank looks crowded to the eye.
Overfeeding compounds both problems at once: uneaten food decays into the
same ammonia the fish themselves produce, so a tank fed twice what it needs
is quietly running two tanks' worth of biological load through one
colony's capacity.
