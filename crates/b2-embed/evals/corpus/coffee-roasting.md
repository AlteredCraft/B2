title: Coffee roasting

Raw green beans tumble in a heated rotating drum, drying out, turning yellow,
then browning as sugars caramelize. A few minutes in they audibly pop — the
first crack — the point where a light profile can be stopped; letting the
development run longer darkens the bean, trading origin acidity for bitter,
smoky depth. Overshooting scorches the surface and the batch tastes burnt.

## Green coffee

Unroasted seed arrives dense, grassy-smelling, and stable for a year or more in
a cool room, which is why the trade ships it green and roasts it near the
customer. Density is the property that matters most at the machine: high-grown
lots from steep volcanic slopes are harder and slower to conduct heat inward,
so they tolerate — and need — more aggressive early energy than a soft lowland
bean that will scorch under the same flame.

Moisture content sits between ten and twelve percent on arrival. Below that the
seed has dried out in storage and behaves erratically, browning at the surface
while the core lags; above it the batch stalls, soaking up energy without
climbing in temperature. Screen size and shape matter for the same reason. A
mixed lot with peaberries and flats together will never roast evenly, because
the small round seeds finish while the large flat ones are still developing.

## The roast curve

The operator's real instrument is not the flame but the bean-temperature probe,
and the shape of its trace over time is the profile. Everything else — drum
speed, airflow, batch size — is a lever for bending that curve.

Charging a warm drum with room-temperature seed drops the reading sharply, then
it bottoms out and begins to climb; that reversal is the turning point, and it
should arrive within the first ninety seconds. A turning point that comes too
late means the drum was charged cold or overloaded, and the whole batch will
run behind schedule from there.

The drying phase that follows consumes most of the water and roughly half the
total time while producing almost no flavor. It is a heat-transfer problem, not
a chemistry one, and the goal is simply to arrive at the browning stage with
energy still in reserve. Push too hard here and the surface dehydrates ahead of
the core, which sets up a hollow, papery cup no later adjustment can rescue.

Browning begins near 150 °C, when the Maillard reaction starts building the
brown pigments and the hundreds of aromatic compounds that make the drink smell
like coffee rather than hay. The seed swells, the chaff loosens, and the smell
in the room shifts from cut grass to baking bread. From here the curve should
still be rising, but the rate of rise should be steadily falling — a curve that
flattens completely stalls the roast, and one that climbs at a constant rate
runs away and bakes the sugars past where they are pleasant.

| Milestone | Bean temperature | Elapsed | What it sounds and smells like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Charge | 200 °C drum | 0:00 | Hiss of cool seed hitting hot steel |
| Turning point | 88 °C | 1:00–1:30 | Quiet; the probe reverses and starts climbing |
| Yellowing | 150 °C | 4:00–5:00 | Cut grass gives way to toasted bread |
| First crack | 196 °C | 8:30–9:30 | Sharp irregular snapping, like popcorn starting |
| Light finish | 205 °C | 9:30–10:30 | Snapping thins out; sweet, bright, acidic |
| Second crack | 224 °C | 12:00–13:00 | Fainter, faster crackle; oils bead on the surface |

Reading the table across rather than down is the useful skill: the intervals
between milestones say more than the absolute numbers, because every machine's
probe sits in a slightly different spot and reads a few degrees off. Two roasts
that hit the same marks at the same minutes will taste alike even when their
thermometers disagree.

Sample roasting is the parallel practice that makes any of this decidable. A
few hundred grams go through a tiny drum on a fixed profile, get ground, and are
brewed side by side in identical bowls, crust broken and slurped from a spoon so
the taster moves through a dozen cups in minutes. Buyers score the lot on
sweetness, body, acidity, and defects before a single sack changes hands, and
the roaster repeats the exercise afterward to check that the production profile
actually delivered what the sample promised.

## Development time

The stretch between the onset of snapping and the moment the batch drops is
where the roast is finally decided, and expressing it as a share of total time
makes profiles comparable across machines and batch sizes. A fifth to a quarter
is the conventional window. Below it the sugars have not finished, and the cup
comes up sharp, savory, and thin no matter how sweet the lot; above it the
distinguishing character of the origin flattens into generic roast, and the
difference between an exceptional farm and a commodity blend disappears.

## Defects

An underdeveloped roast is the most common fault and the hardest to recognize
without a reference, because the color looks perfectly acceptable while the
interior is still raw. The cup tastes grassy, sour rather than bright, and
oddly salty, with a drying finish; grinding the beans and smelling the powder
gives it away faster than looking at them, since the aroma stays green.

Baking is the opposite failure and comes from a curve that flattened and then
was chased back up with more heat. The sugars caramelize slowly without the
aromatics developing alongside, and the result is dull, bready, and papery —
technically brown, entirely flat. Scorching and tipping are mechanical rather
than chemical: dark spots on the flat face mean the seed sat against metal that
was too hot, and burnt pinpricks at the tips mean the early energy was far too
aggressive for the batch size.

## Resting and storage

Freshly dropped coffee is saturated with carbon dioxide and brews badly, the
grounds foaming up and the water channeling through the puck. Three to seven
days of rest in a valved bag lets the gas escape while keeping oxygen out, and
the window of genuinely good drinking runs a few weeks past that before the
oils stale. Whole beans hold far longer than ground, cold storage helps only if
the container is sealed against condensation, and the freezer is a real option
for long-term keeping provided the bag is never opened warm.
