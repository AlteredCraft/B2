## Pour-over

Hot water poured in stages over a paper filter and a bed of medium-fine
grounds, drawing through by gravity alone rather than a pressurized shot or
a long soak. A brief bloom — thirty to forty-five seconds with just enough
water to wet the bed — lets trapped gas escape before the main pour, so
the extraction that follows doesn't fight the outgassing. Pour rate
controls strength more than any dial: a slow, thin stream held near the
center gives water more contact time per gram and lands sweeter and more
concentrated, while a fast pour that floods the bed and drains quickly
gives a thinner, brighter cup. Grind size adjusts against pour rate — too
fine and the bed chokes, water pools on top and the flow crawls to a stop;
too open and it runs through before extraction properly starts, tasting
sharp no matter how carefully the water went in. The paper takes out the
sediment an unfiltered cup leaves behind, at the cost of most of the oils,
which is the trade the method is chosen for.

## Centering on the wheel

Centering is the one skill that makes every later step possible and the
one that eats most of a beginner's studio hours: a ball of clay slapped
slightly off the wheel head's middle will wobble the entire time it spins,
and no amount of careful pulling afterward fixes a wall that started from
an uneven base. The trick is less about strength than about steady,
braced pressure — elbows locked against the hips rather than floating in
the air — while the hands squeeze inward and slightly down until the spinning
mass stops flickering at the edges and reads as a single still cylinder.
Opening the centered lump means pressing a thumb straight down into the
middle, leaving an even floor thick enough not to crack away from the
walls later, then widening that hole with fingers braced against each
other from the inside and outside at once. Pulling the wall up is a
repeated squeeze-and-glide from base to rim, thinning and rising a little
each pass rather than trying to reach final height in one go; pull too
fast and the wall goes thin and wavers, pull too slow with too much
compression and the clay tires and starts to slump instead of climbing.
A rim that flares or wobbles at the very end is almost always fixed by
slowing the wheel rather than pressing harder — most beginner mistakes
come from fighting the spin instead of matching it.
